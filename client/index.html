A phone already feels how you move

Smartphones and smartwatches carry an accelerometer and a gyroscope that measure motion continuously. Turning those streams into a label such as “walking” or “sitting” is human activity recognition (HAR). It powers fitness and calorie tracking, fall detection for the elderly, rehabilitation monitoring, sleep analysis and phones that adapt to what you are doing.

Classic pipelines hand-craft hundreds of statistics per window (means, energy, FFT coefficients) and feed them to an SVM or random forest. They work, but they need expert knowledge and must be redesigned for every new sensor or placement. This project lets a neural network learn the features directly from the raw signal.

The task

Given a 2.56-second window of body acceleration, angular velocity and total acceleration from a waist-mounted phone, choose one of six activities.

Why it is hard

  1. Signals are noisy and differ between people in gait, speed and phone placement.
  2. Some classes are almost twins: upstairs and downstairs, sitting and standing.
  3. The model must stay small enough for a phone or an embedded board.

Objectives

  1. Study the theory of CNNs and LSTMs, plus transfer learning, attention and GANs, as they apply to sensor time series.
  2. Build an end-to-end CNN-LSTM in TensorFlow/Keras that learns from raw inertial signals, with no hand-made features.
  3. Train it with sound regularisation: batch normalisation, dropout, early stopping, learning-rate scheduling and a stratified validation split.
  4. Evaluate on unseen subjects with accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and the confusion matrix.
  5. Analyse errors by class and by subject, then propose concrete improvements.

The data: 30 volunteers, one phone on the waist

The UCI Human Activity Recognition dataset (Anguita et al., 2013) records a Samsung Galaxy S II at 50 Hz while volunteers aged 19–48 perform six activities. The signals are noise-filtered, total acceleration is split into body and gravity parts, and everything is cut into fixed windows.

Cutting the stream into windows

Each window is 128 samples (2.56 s). Consecutive windows overlap by 50%, so every moment is seen twice. A window becomes a 128 × 9 tensor: body acceleration xyz, body gyroscope xyz, total acceleration xyz.

Preprocessing

  1. Shift labels to 0–5.
  2. Hold out 15% of the training windows as validation, stratified by class.
  3. Standardise each channel to zero mean and unit variance using training statistics only.
  4. Save the mean and standard deviation (norm.npz) and reuse them for validation, test and live inference, so nothing leaks from the test set.

Dataset facts

Class balance

The classes are close to balanced, so accuracy is a meaningful headline number. Macro-F1 is reported next to it.

The model: local patterns first, then their rhythm

Why a hybrid

CNN stage. Each 1-D filter spans 0.1 s across all nine channels and learns motion primitives such as a heel strike. Weight sharing means the pattern is found wherever it occurs. Two pooling steps shrink 128 time steps to 32 while the feature depth grows to 128.

LSTM stage. Two stacked LSTMs read those 32 feature vectors in order. Gates decide what to remember, so the network can tell the asymmetric cadence of climbing from descending.

Regularisation. Batch normalisation, dropout 0.3, early stopping and learning-rate decay keep the train and validation loss almost identical.

Layer summary

Training configuration

Training accuracy climbs from about 77% after epoch one to about 96%, validation settles near 95.8%, and the two losses converge (≈0.09 and ≈0.085). Validation windows come from the same 21 subjects as training, so this is optimistic compared with the unseen test subjects.

For context, the dataset authors reported about 96% with an SVM on 561 hand-made features. Reaching 92.77% from raw signals alone is competitive but not yet better than the strongest feature-based baselines.

Where the model goes wrong

There are 213 errors in total. They are concentrated, which makes them fixable.

Most frequent confusions

Sitting versus standing: 80% of all errors

Both are static, so body acceleration and gyroscope are near zero. The only reliable cue is the phone’s orientation against gravity, which is subtle after per-channel standardisation and changes with how each person wears the phone. Standing is mistaken for sitting more often than the reverse, so the decision boundary leans towards sitting.

Upstairs versus downstairs: 12%

Both are periodic gaits that differ in vertical acceleration and cadence, which varies between people. Twenty-five upstairs windows were labelled downstairs and none the other way round, hinting at a subject-specific bias.

The failures are personal

Five subjects (12, 13, 18, 20, 24) are classified at 99.7–100%, while subject 10 (72.4%) and subject 9 (80.2%) carry a large share of the errors. A single accuracy number hides how differently the model treats different people.

Confidence is a warning light

Mean softmax confidence is 0.964 on correct predictions and 0.806 on wrong ones, so low-confidence windows can be flagged or smoothed over neighbouring windows.
